Purchase at least one, though may need a second before the end of the course.  I will not micro manage your lab notebooks, but state there are many philosophies on this.  Some believe that this must be maintained as a legal document, others that it is a guide to enable others (or you) to follow your work, or last that it is a document to assist you and you alone in keeping a record for yourself.  My own philosophy falls into the latter two categories.  However if you work in industry you will need to learn to keep a legal styled notebook.  

 

Here is a good link on lab note books and lab report writing.  http://www.drjbloom.com/Public%20files/Laboratory%20Notebook%20Booklet.pdf

 

Your lab notebooks must include:

1.     Table of contents (leave 2-3 pages at the beginning so you can fit this for the labs).  

a.      The table will refer to lab title and page number

2.     Start a new lab with title, your name, and date.

a.      Continue with that lab by writing up a summary of lab notes and procedures prior to entering lab.

                                                    i.     You can have a lab handout with you (not in the lab notebook)

                                                  ii.     If it is clear that you are not prepared for lab----not ready to do lab---it may prompt a quick notebook check and comments in lab notebook. 

b.     I do not want you to keep a library of reference materials pasted or taped into your lab notebooks.   What is in your lab book is of your creation---not mine, not APS, not AJP, not other journals.  

c.       

3.     Include data

a.      Data includes noting any experimental conditions that may impact the experiment

b.     Sketch experimental setup

c.      You may take picture and leave room for sketch later

d.     For data points that number in thousands---you may refer to your copy of electronic file. (name and location reminder for yourself)

e.      For smaller data sets ---you must have all data in a table in your notebooks

f.      I should be able to find an estimated uncertainty with any directly measured quantity. 

                                                    i.     A data table will typically include a + / - “number” to indicate uncertainty.

                                                  ii.     You may wait till later in your analysis section to explain how you are handling error /uncertainty propagation in your end result.

g.     If you need to retake data—note the time again, and any new conditions.

 

4.     Rough analysis

a.      If there is a method of quick check on data analysis possible then  you can do as you take data.   Do so.   This is the number that says---“OK—my data looks good, and I have a functional experiment with reasonable results”.   If you left lab without doing such a check, you may not catch a critical error.

b.     Most labs have such a “back of the envelope” numbers check available. 

c.      The rough analysis is not part of your later handed in work or results, but is a critical component of your lab notebooks.

5.     Results

a.      You are permitted to give final results or to make a permanent 1 page to 1 page copy of completed graphs, (speed of light plot, spectra plots, etc).

b.     You must describe a methodology of analysis (how did you get your end result from your data).

c.      If you are comparing your results to a well known standard then you should have that standard in your lab notebook (speed of light).

d.     References are needed even in lab notebooks—especially---

                                                    i.     Since I forbid you to paste full copies of reference materials in lab notebooks, you must provide references to maintain integrity.

                                                  ii.     “Colbert’s old Advanced lab handout from 1898”,   “Arthur Schawlow’s AJP paper from…….”.   

                                                iii.     If you include information without reference, I may call you in to re-derive or explain where that information comes from (no notes).

e.      Regardless of having a plot/graph---you must include the primary final results of the experiment---leave room.

f.      The final result you have listed in your lab book should agree with what is in your handed in analysis and your lab reports.   This includes writing resulting numbers out properly with proper significant figures and uncertainties.

6.     Never

a.     Never erase, cross out only.

b.     Do leave space to complete the lab work as you start on next lab.

7.     Notebook Collection

a.      Most likely at the end of 3rd lab, and 6th lab

b.     Instructions to follow.

 

 

The list above is not comprehensive but should get you good lab notebook grades.  Remember the purpose is to have a notebook which ultimately allows you to go back in five years and do the lab on your own, and also know the results of this lab.     

 

Do not erase anything. If you have errors, then cross out and correct or cross out and redo (justify this). Justify everything. If you retake data, and throw out old data, have a reason!

Note anything unusual beyond the scope of the requested observations or tasks.

Note how your measurements are related to what you are trying to determine (i.e., why are you making these measurements).  Do at least a preliminary analysis (i.e. if linear relation get a rough slope from first and last points....).      Compare to standard or known results (if available).    

 

Observations and Data collection can only be made while you are doing the experiment!

YOU MUST ESTIMATE THE EXPERIMENTAL UNCERTAINTIES IN YOUR EQUIPMENT (HOW WELL CAN YOU READ, AND HOW RELIABLE ARE THE NUMBERS FROM YOUR EQUIPMENT).
